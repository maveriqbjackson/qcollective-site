Colorado's new AI law, in plain English

You now have a right to know when a computer helped turn you down. On November 19 the Attorney General adopted the rules that decide how that right works. Here is what we asked for, and what we got.

Picture an apartment application in Colorado. A screening company runs your rental history, court records and credit through its software, and you come back as a bottom-quartile risk. The landlord says no. You pay the application fee again at the next place, and the place after that, and nobody ever tells you a score was involved.

Colorado decided that shouldn't happen anymore. This spring the legislature passed two laws about artificial intelligence, and the governor signed both on May 14, 2026. They take effect on January 1, 2027.

Update: the rules were adopted November 19

The Colorado Department of Law adopted the final rules, 4 CCR 904-6, on Thursday, November 19, 2026. The comment period is closed. Next, the Attorney General issues a formal opinion on the rules. Then they're filed with the Secretary of State, and they become final 20 days after they're published.

The two laws

The Automated Decision-Making Technology Act (SB 26-189) covers what lawyers call consequential decisions: housing, jobs, credit, health care, education. When an automated system plays a real part in a decision that goes against you, you are owed a notice. That notice has to say an automated system was used, give the main reasons, tell you what data it relied on, explain how to correct that data, and tell you how to get a human to review it.

The Conversational Artificial Intelligence Services Act (HB 26-1263) covers the chatbots people talk to. Its rules were adopted in the same set. Among other things, a chatbot talking with a minor has to keep a notice on the screen the whole time saying it is AI and not a person, and companies have to report each year on how their chatbots handle signs of suicidal thinking or self-harm. Our comment focused on the first law, because that is where a person's housing, paycheck or care is on the line.

The Attorney General enforces both. There is no private right to sue under the decision law, which means the rules the Attorney General writes, and the records they require companies to keep, are most of what stands behind your rights.

How the three fights came out

The law sets the outline. The Attorney General's office filled in the details in a rule set called 4 CCR 904-6. Most of the comments on file came from the companies that have to follow it. Here is where the final rules landed.

1. When does the law even apply? Mostly not adopted

The law kicks in when an automated system "materially influences" a decision, meaning it was more than a trivial factor. The final rules do something good first: if a score, rank or recommendation about you was looked at during the decision, it is presumed to have influenced it, and the company has to prove otherwise.

But the rules then define a trivial factor as one that is "not a substantial factor," and say it can count as trivial "if other relevant factors independent of the ADMT output played a significantly larger role." That is the second reading, the one we asked them not to adopt. Go back to the apartment. The landlord points to a gap in your rental history, says it weighed more than the score, and has a way to argue you are owed nothing.

2. Do they have to name who supplied the data? Adopted

Yes. When you ask what data was used against you, the company has 15 days to answer, and it has to name each source: the specific data broker, court, database, school or employer. It can't hide behind "a consumer reporting agency." It is presumed to know any source it got data from directly or through a vendor. If a data broker got your record from somewhere else, the company has to name both when it knows them. You can write to a name. You can't write to a category.

3. Is the human review real? Partly

The reviewer has to have real power to change the decision, can't be steered by upper management, and has to be protected from retaliation. Automated tools that use machine learning or large language models can't help with the review. Plain rule-following tools, and tools that only summarize, organize or translate, still can. Those parts held.

What didn't hold: the rule that the reviewer can't be a subordinate of whoever made the original call is gone. And the final rules say a review that never reverses anything does not, on its own, prove the review wasn't real.

The timing fight Adopted

There's no grace period in the final rules. And an old federal credit notice only counts if it also carries everything Colorado's law requires, sent within 30 days of the decision. Notices have to go out through every channel the company normally uses to reach you, not just one.

What we asked for, and what we got

On October 2, The Q Collective filed a 9-page comment for the people on the other end of these decisions. We have no client and no money in the outcome.

  • Adopted Companies have to name each actual source of your data.
  • Adopted No grace period, and federal notices only count if they carry everything Colorado requires.
  • Adopted Notices go out through every channel the company normally uses with you.
  • Partly Human review: real authority and no AI help held, but the "not the boss's subordinate" rule was cut.
  • Close Answer time: we asked for 10 business days, and the rule is 15 days.
  • Mostly not adopted When the law applies: a presumption we wanted is in, but "trivial" now means "not a substantial factor," the reading we argued against.

What you can do now

  1. Know the signs. If you're turned down for housing, a job, credit, care or school, and a score, ranking or screening tool was part of it, you're owed a written notice within 30 days once the law is in force.
  2. Ask what was used. The notice has to tell you how to ask for the details, including the name of the tool and every source of your data. They have 15 days to answer.
  3. Fix what's wrong. Once you know who supplied a bad record, you can ask them to correct it and ask the company to look at your case again with the right information.
  4. Ask for a human. You can ask for a real person to review the decision, one with the power to change it.
  5. Keep everything. Save every denial letter and email. If something feels wrong, tell us. Real stories are how rules get fixed.

What happens next

The Attorney General issues a formal opinion on the rules. Then they're filed with the Secretary of State and become final 20 days after they're published. That date isn't set yet. It depends on when the opinion comes, so it may not line up with January 1. The law itself sets January 1, 2027 for its main duties to start. We'll post the rules' final date here the day it's known.

The "substantial factor" definition is now in the rules. Changing it would take the legislature, or a later rulemaking. We'll keep track of all of it on our timeline and in the Sunday Update.
