CESA in plain English

Our flagship bill is 431 pages long. Here is what it actually does, how it's paid for, and the honest hard parts.

The Colorado Everyday Stability Act starts from one idea. When a family's health, income, housing and food are steady, everything else gets easier, for them and for the state.

CESA is a full, written bill, not a wish list. The current master is version 16.3, published October 23, 2026, and it runs 447 pages. In version 16.3 the bill took its new name, the Colorado Everyday Stability Act, and the letters stayed the same. Every version starts with a list of exactly what changed, so anyone can follow how it got here. Read the whole thing (PDF).

What it would do for a family

Healthcare: Care Without Fear

One statewide health system, and the goal is full single-payer. Here is how it gets there. Care Without Fear launches as a public option: anyone can join, and anyone can keep private insurance instead. The switch to single-payer happens only when Colorado voters approve it at a statewide election, with a target of Year 3. After that, private insurance becomes extra coverage on top. What you pay is a slice of your income, on a sliding scale:

  • Below 138% of the Colorado Cost of Living Standard: $0
  • Then 2%, 4%, 5% and 6% as income rises
  • The top band pays 7%, capped at $15,000 a year

The bill's own comparison puts a typical family's yearly savings against today's premiums in the thousands, often more than $10,000.

Childcare

Free for families below 100% of the Cost of Living Standard. Above that, families pay 3%, 5% or 7% of income depending on their band, and never more than 10%.

Housing, work and the rest

The seven pillars run through the whole bill: Health, Income, Family, Housing, Food, Economic Opportunity, and Protection from Disruption. That covers a plan to end homelessness, help for workers pushed out by AI, a state bank, veterans' programs, a cap on predatory loans, broadband treated as a basic utility, and more. It's a lot. That's on purpose.

How it's paid for

CESA runs on enterprise funds. That's the same kind of structure Colorado already uses for things like unemployment insurance. People and businesses pay in and get specific services back. The main money sources:

  • The sliding-scale healthcare fee above.
  • An employer healthcare assessment that replaces premiums. Businesses with under $500,000 in payroll pay nothing. Above that it's 6%, 8% or 9% of payroll depending on size. The bill estimates that's roughly 30 to 40% less than what those employers pay in premiums today.
  • Hospital and insurance-company assessments, plus savings from cutting paperwork.

The honest part about taxes

The healthcare core is built on fees, with no income tax increase. But CESA is not tax-free, and I'm not going to pretend it is. Alongside the fees, it proposes four health-impact taxes: 5 cents per ounce on sugary drinks, and extra sales taxes of 8% on high-sugar, high-fat foods, 5% on ultra-processed foods and 3% on chain-restaurant meals. The bill estimates they'd raise $630 to $870 million a year, and puts that money toward healthcare and making healthy food cheaper. Version 16.3 adds a 21% sales tax on alcohol, with the money going first to mental health and addiction care. Here's the rule I won't bend on: every one of those taxes goes to Colorado voters as its own ballot question, so you can say yes to some and no to others. That holds even if TABOR ever goes away. An income tax shows up only as a last-resort backup, and only if voters approve it.

Nothing switches on by itself

This is the part people miss. No program starts automatically. Each one has to pass an independent money check first (an actuarial certification), prove its funding is actually coming in, show it has the staff and systems ready, and get a formal sign-off. A permanent, nonpartisan council of 11 reviews the whole thing, publishes plain-language reports, and puts every program through a review every five years.

The hard parts

  • It's big. The bill's own estimate is about $9.8 billion in Year 1. The bill's case is that much of it replaces what families and employers already spend on premiums. Either way, it's a big lift.
  • "Voluntary" has fine print. Employers are in by default. They can opt out each year and keep their own coverage, but they give up CESA's benefits and preferences if they do. Once voters approve single-payer, Care Without Fear becomes the system for everyone.
  • It will draw a single-subject challenge. Colorado bills have to cover one subject. CESA's subject is life stability for Colorado residents, and every section is written to serve it.

Why it stays one bill

I get asked to break CESA into pieces all the time. I won't. The pieces lean on each other. Healthcare savings help pay for the rest, and stable housing makes healthcare cheaper. Almost every piece is already working somewhere in the world. What CESA does is put those proven pieces together into one plan built to last. Split it up and you get seven half-measures that each lose on their own.
