Our scorecard on Colorado's AI rules

On October 2 we filed nine pages of comments on the rules. On November 19 the final rules came out. Here's what we asked for, what made it in, and what didn't.

We said we'd show our work. So here it is: a public scorecard on our own filing, the wins and the miss, and what we're doing about the miss.

The law, in one paragraph

This spring Colorado passed a law on automated decisions (SB 26-189). If a computer score or ranking was more than a trivial factor in turning you down for housing, a job, credit, health care or school, you're owed a notice. It has to tell you a system was used, what data it relied on, how to fix that data, and how to get a real person to look at your case. The law sets the outline. The Attorney General's rules fill in the details. Most of the comments on those rules came from the companies that have to follow them. We filed for the people on the other end, with no client and no money in the outcome. The full explainer is here.

What made it in

  • The real name of your data source. Not "a consumer reporting agency." The actual data broker, court or employer. You ask, and they have 15 days to answer. Federal regulators found 1 in 5 people had an error on a credit report, and 4 in 5 who disputed one got it changed. You can write to a name. You can't write to a category.
  • No grace period. And an old federal credit notice only counts if it carries everything Colorado requires.
  • Every channel. Notices go out through every channel the company normally uses to reach you, not just the one you're least likely to check.

What partly made it in

Human review mostly held. The person reviewing your case has to have real power to change it, can't be steered from above, and can't be punished for overruling the system. The review can't be handed back to another AI. One piece was cut: the rule that the reviewer can't report to the person who made the original call.

What didn't

The law applies when the system was more than a trivial ("de minimis") factor. The rules do something good first: if a score was looked at, it's presumed to count. But then they define trivial as "not a substantial factor," and say a factor can be trivial when other factors "played a significantly larger role" (4 CCR 904-6, Rule 2.2). That raises the bar the legislature set.

What that looks like

A renter is scored by screening software and turned down. The landlord says a gap in her rental history mattered more than the score. Under this rule, she may never learn she was scored, which source had a wrong record, or how to fix it. Same software. Same denial.

What we're doing about it

We're asking lawmakers to carry a one-sentence fix in the 2027 session, which opens January 11. It adds this to the law's definition:

An output of an automated decision-making technology is not a de minimis factor solely because one or more other factors had a greater effect on the outcome of the consequential decision.

No new agency, no new fund, no new cost. It keeps the line where the legislature drew it. The legislature also has a committee that checks whether new rules go beyond what a law allows, and this rule is a fair one to look at. We'll post every step on Our work.

What you can do

If you get turned down for housing, a job, a loan or care in 2027, save the letter. Ask what data was used. Ask who supplied it. Then tell us what happened. Real stories are how rules get fixed.
